## Abstract

Most discussion of AI in software development sits at one of two
extremes: AI as autocomplete, or AI as autonomous agent. This article
describes a middle ground I have been practicing — AI-Human pair
programming with cross-vetting — and argues that its closest legal
analog is supervised paid-intern work, not contracted labor or
software tooling. Under that framing, much of what looks like novel
AI compliance is structurally already handled by existing
trainee-supervision law. I show the methodology in use through six
public pull requests, examine a specific case where an AI reviewer
caught a correctness regression in code another AI proposed, and a
counterpart case where a human caught long-standing dead code that
both AI tiers had walked past. I close with where the analogy holds,
where it breaks, and what a 2026-current legal framework
(Texas's HB 149, plus the NIST AI Risk Management Framework) means
for adopters.

## §1. The missing middle

There are two stories about AI in software development, and they're
both incomplete.

The first story is **AI as autocomplete**. The human drives, the AI
suggests tokens, the human accepts or rejects. This is the story
told by Copilot's marketing in 2022 and by skeptics today who
distrust AI's role in regulated work. It is technically true for
inline-completion use cases and entirely wrong for any AI tool that
proposes a multi-file change, opens a pull request, or responds
to a code review. The autocomplete frame doesn't fit the way
practitioners actually use Claude Code, Cursor, or Cline.

The second story is **AI as autonomous agent**. The AI ships
unsupervised. This is the story told by ambitious agentic-coding
demos and by regulators worried about high-risk AI under the EU AI
Act. It is technically true for some narrow agentic deployments and
wrong for any production codebase under serious change-management
discipline. No org I have worked with — nor any I would want to —
runs autonomous merge for non-trivial code.

Most actual AI-assisted development sits between the two. The AI
proposes a change. An automated reviewer (a separate AI tool, often
from a different vendor) reviews it. A human reads both, runs the
build, and decides. Three independent perspectives, one accountable
human, full audit trail through pull-request comments and tool
conversation transcripts.

I want to argue that this middle ground has a clean legal analog
that the ongoing regulatory conversation has not named: **the AI is
working as a paid code intern**.

Like a paid intern, the AI is supervised, has a reduced standard of
care, produces output that vests in the employer rather than itself,
and depends on a senior reviewer to make the final call. Unlike a
contractor, the AI carries no independent professional duty. Unlike
a tool, the AI makes meaningful interpretive decisions that shape
the output. Trainee law was written for exactly this shape — it
just hasn't been pointed at AI yet.

The "AI as intern" metaphor is already in informal circulation —
legal-tech vendors describe their tools as "tireless junior
associates"; engineering blogs treat AI assistants as "code
interns." My contribution is not the metaphor itself but the
structural mapping onto trainee-supervision law and the operational
methodology that goes with it.

The rest of this article spells out what that means in practice.
§2 describes the methodology. §3 walks through a worked example. §4
makes the legal claim concrete via the affirmative defense Texas
codified on January 1, 2026. §4.5 explains why the trainee framing
fits code review specifically. §5 names where the analogy breaks.
§6 covers failure modes. §7 is the adoption section. §8 closes with
open questions.

I am writing as a single practitioner reflecting on recent work,
not as a researcher reporting a controlled study. §5 names the
limits this imposes on the claims.

## §2. The practice

The methodology is short. It has four required controls and one
workflow that connects them:

1. **The human is the named approver on every change.** Branch
   protection enforces this. No AI bot — proposer or reviewer —
   can self-merge. This is the hard line that distinguishes the
   methodology from autonomous agent deployments.
2. **An independent automated reviewer is configured.** Different
   vendor or model from the proposer where possible. This reduces
   the monoculture risk that occurs when the proposer and reviewer
   share training data and therefore share blind spots.
3. **Conversation transcripts are retained.** The proposer-side AI
   conversations are kept, not just the resulting commits. This is
   evidence the cross-vet happened.
4. **Critical-path exceptions are documented.** Some changes —
   security-sensitive code, financial calculations, life-safety
   logic — get a stricter review path declared in `CONTRIBUTING.md`
   or equivalent.

The workflow these controls operate within looks like this:

```
Human or AI proposes change
  → AI proposer drafts change + rationale, opens PR
    → AI reviewer runs automated review, posts findings
      → Human reads diff + AI proposer's reasoning + AI reviewer's findings
        → Human fills pair-review notes (both sides — see §2.1)
          → Human decides: merge / request changes / reject
```

The visual form is a single diagram with three roles, four audit
artifacts, and one decision point [Figure 1, see workflow-figure.md].
The methodology is short to describe, and the brevity is a feature.
The interesting work is in what the audit trail does *between* the
PR and the human's decision.

### §2.1. The pair-review table

Conventional code review tools have a slot for the human reviewer to
comment on the author's code. They do not have a slot for the AI
proposer to comment on the prompts that drove the implementation. I
think this is a defect of the tooling, not a property of the work.

The methodology's pair-review table is per-PR, with two sections.

**Section A — AI notes about human prompts.** What the AI flagged
about the direction it was given: ambiguity, multiple matches,
conflicts with prior instructions, risk flags, scope expansions,
suggested refinements.

**Section B — Human notes about AI implementation.** Standard code
review, named explicitly: style or convention deviations, logic
concerns, coverage gaps, performance, security, deferred
disagreements, approved-as-is observations.

Both sections must be non-empty for the pair-review to count. If
Section A is empty, the AI didn't surface ambiguity (re-read the
conversation; things probably got flagged that weren't recorded). If
Section B is empty, the human didn't engage substantively with the
output (re-read the diff; "looks fine" is not a review).

The pair-review table is not the only audit artifact. PR threads,
commit messages, conversation transcripts, AI reviewer comments,
merge commits with `Co-Authored-By` trailers — all of these matter.
But the pair-review table is the artifact that makes the *pair*
in pair programming structurally visible. Without it, AI's
contribution disappears into the noise of "human author with AI
assist."

I will return to the audit-artifact framework in §7.

### §2.2. What this is not

The methodology is not about replacing skilled human review. It
adds a layer; it does not remove the layer that was already there.
A team that ships unreviewed AI output and labels it
"cross-vetting" because Codex saw the diff is doing something
weaker than the methodology and shouldn't get the benefit of
calling it by the same name.

The methodology is also not about specific tool choices. I happen
to use Claude Code as the proposer and Codex as the reviewer; the
methodology runs equally well with Cursor + CodeRabbit, or any
proposer-reviewer pair where the two come from independent vendors.
What matters is that the proposer-tier and reviewer-tier are
substantively independent, not that they have specific brand names.

## §3. A worked example

The cleanest demonstration of the methodology working in the recent
sessions on `retirement-dashboard-angular` is the trace from PR #58
to PR #59. The repository implements a Monte Carlo retirement
calculator; PR #58 added a feature, PR #59 fixed a regression in it
that the AI reviewer caught.

[PR #58 — feat(guardrails): essential vs discretionary spending
split](https://github.com/justice8096/retirement-dashboard-angular/pull/58)
landed on 2026-04-25 at 08:50 UTC. Among other things, it touched
`src/app/lib/monte-carlo.ts` to add a per-trial Long-Term Care roll:

```typescript
// Per-trial LTC roll. Resolves once at trial start; deterministic
// across years within the trial. willNeedLtc: 70% of 65+ Americans
// by Genworth.
if (ltcSelfInsure && oldestAge0 != null
    && Math.random() < ltcProbability) {
  const ltcStartAge = ltcStartAgeMin
    + Math.random() * (ltcStartAgeMax - ltcStartAgeMin);
  // ...
}
```

Per-trial randomness is fine when you run thousands of trials and
look at the distribution. The Monte Carlo tool supports several
modes, though, and one of them — `historical-sequence` — runs as
a single deterministic path through historical returns. Adding a
coin flip into a single-path simulation is a category error.

Codex flagged this as a P2 finding during automated review of #58,
posted as an inline comment on `monte-carlo.ts:583`:

> **Prevent random LTC shock in single-path historical mode**
>
> This new LTC roll adds randomness per run, but historical-sequence
> mode still executes as a single run, so enabling self-insure LTC
> turns the result into one random draw rather than a stable
> estimate. In practice, identical inputs can flip between "LTC
> happened" and "LTC didn't happen," causing success rate and
> percentiles to jump unpredictably instead of reflecting the
> configured LTC probability.

The finding correctly identifies the mechanism (`Math.random()` runs
once per trial, `historical-sequence` only runs one trial), the
user-visible symptom (identical inputs producing different results),
and the semantic break (the result fails to "reflect the configured
LTC probability" even though it nominally uses it).

PR #58 merged with this P2 still open — a deliberate decision to
land the feature and fix the regression in a follow-up. About thirty
minutes later, [PR #59 — fix(monte-carlo): stabilize LTC self-insure
in single-run modes](https://github.com/justice8096/retirement-dashboard-angular/pull/59)
opened with this in its body:

> Addresses **Codex P2 review** on #58 (which had already merged) —
> the per-trial LTC random roll became a coin flip in
> `historical-sequence` mode (`effectiveRuns === 1`), making
> identical inputs flip between "LTC happened" / "didn't" between
> runs.

That sentence is the AI proposer (Claude Code) explicitly attributing
the issue to the AI reviewer's finding. The audit trail names its
source.

The fix switched LTC behavior by mode: random per-trial roll when
multi-run, expected-value when single-run. The PR body included a
math sanity-check:

> Random mode, per-year deduction during LTC window (averaged across
> many trials):
> - `P(LTC) × cost_per_year = 0.7 × $108K = $75,600/yr`
>
> EV mode, per-year deduction during LTC window:
> - `cost_per_year × ltcProbability = $108K × 0.7 = $75,600/yr` ✓

Both arrive at the same `$75,600/yr` expected per-year deduction.
That equality is what makes the fix legitimate: random-mode and
EV-mode produce the same mean, so swapping based on
`effectiveRuns === 1` preserves the simulation's first moment while
killing the noise.

A few things distinguish this case from a generic "AI fixes its own
bug" story. The reviewer found a non-trivial correctness issue —
not a style nit or a typo. The bug doesn't break compilation or
visibly crash; it silently degrades to noise in one mode. That's
the class of issue automated review is supposed to catch and human
review tends to miss because it's mode-conditional. The fix is
mathematically justified, not just plausible-looking — without the
sanity-check, "use EV when single-run" might still drift from the
random-mode mean in subtle ways. And the audit trail is complete:
PR #58 has the inline comment, PR #59's body links back, anyone
walking the trail later can see exactly what was found, who acted
on it, and how the cure was validated.

To put this case in its broader context: across the six origin PRs
(#56-#61) merged on 2026-04-24 and 04-25, the AI reviewer posted
eight inline findings — one P1 and seven P2. Five PRs received
findings; one (#60) was reviewed clean. **Six of the eight were
actioned** in subsequent commits or follow-up PRs (verified
against the resulting code); two were deferred — both PR #56 P2
findings on LTC duration rounding and insurance-premium inflation,
where the human approver chose not to act. This is not a
controlled comparison — no solo-reviewer baseline, no
false-positive count. What it shows is substantive review activity
at 1.33 findings per PR including a P1 severity finding, while
the human approver retained and exercised authority to override
bot recommendations when warranted.

That 1.33 number lands in the same time window as CodeRabbit's
December 2025 study of 470 open-source pull requests. CodeRabbit
found AI-coauthored PRs averaged 10.83 issues each against 6.45 for
human-only PRs — about **1.7× more issues** in AI code, with the
largest gaps in readability (3×), performance (~8× for excessive
I/O patterns), and security (2.74×). Two caveats from their own
methodology section: they could not confirm authorship directly
(classification was heuristic, so some "human" PRs likely had AI
assistance), and "issues" reflect CodeRabbit's vendor taxonomy.
Both are stated in the report.

Even with those caveats, CodeRabbit's framing tracks the
methodology's framing closely: not "AI code is worse" but **"it
needs engineered guardrails."** Their concluding line — "quality
isn't automatic. It requires deliberate engineering. Even when
using AI tools." — is the cross-vet methodology in fewer words.
Read against that baseline, the cross-vet's job is not to verdict
AI capability (CodeRabbit's data does that more empirically than
this article can) but to **surface AI's defect surplus as
reviewable findings before merge** rather than let it ship as
production bugs. The 1.33 finding-per-PR rate is what that
surfacing looks like in one repository.

§5 returns to a counterpart case — PR #60, where the human caught
something both AI tiers walked past. Before that, a question worth
pausing on: if the methodology is structurally defensible, where
does the legal weight actually sit?

## §4. The emerging duty of care

The legal landscape for AI in software development was, until very
recently, mostly guidance and aspiration. NIST published the AI
Risk Management Framework. The EU AI Act phased in over 2024-2026.
US states drafted patchwork rules. None of it gave a working
developer or their counsel a concrete answer to "what does
compliance look like today?"

That changed on January 1, 2026, when Texas's **Responsible
Artificial Intelligence Governance Act** (TRAIGA; H.B. 149, 89th
Legislature) went into effect. TRAIGA is codified into the Texas
Business & Commerce Code, Chapter 552. The relevant provisions for
AI use in software development sit in **§ 552.104** (60-day notice
and cure period) and **§ 552.105** ("Civil Penalty; Injunction"),
which between them establish three layered protections: a general
rebuttable presumption of reasonable care (§ 552.105(c)), a 60-day
cure window (§ 552.104), and a discovery-and-cure defense to civil
penalty actions (§ 552.105(e)).

The discovery-and-cure defense in § 552.105(e) reads in full:

> A defendant in an action under this section may not be found
> liable if (1) another person uses the artificial intelligence
> system affiliated with the defendant in a manner prohibited by
> this chapter; or (2) the defendant discovers a violation of
> this chapter through:
>
> **(A)** feedback from a developer, deployer, or other person who
> believes a violation has occurred;
>
> **(B)** testing, including adversarial testing or red-team
> testing;
>
> **(C)** following guidelines set by applicable state agencies;
> or
>
> **(D)** if the defendant substantially complies with the most
> recent version of the "Artificial Intelligence Risk Management
> Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile" published
> by the National Institute of Standards and Technology or
> another nationally or internationally recognized risk management
> framework for artificial intelligence systems, an internal
> review process.

Notice the architecture. NIST compliance is **not** itself the
defense. NIST compliance — specifically with NIST AI 600-1, the
Generative AI Profile — is what qualifies an "internal review
process" as a recognized discovery mechanism under (D). The
defense is for *discovering and curing* violations through any of
the four qualifying paths.

Most secondary legal commentary has been less precise —
describing TRAIGA as a general "NIST AI RMF affirmative defense"
without the discovery-and-cure architecture or the alternative-
framework escape hatch. Primary-source verification surfaced the
discrepancy during this article's preparation, which is exactly
the kind of finding the cross-vet methodology is designed to
enable.

The relevant question for adopters: does the cross-vet methodology
satisfy any of the qualifying discovery mechanisms? It satisfies
multiple:

| Statute trigger | Methodology element |
|----------------|---------------------|
| **(A)** Feedback from developer, deployer, or other person | The AI reviewer (Codex or equivalent) is structurally a feedback mechanism. Inline review comments surface violations before merge. |
| **(B)** Testing, including adversarial / red-team | Pre-merge build, lint, test runs; explicit adversarial testing where applicable. |
| **(C)** State agency guidelines | Adoption-time consideration; jurisdiction-dependent. |
| **(D)** Internal review process plus NIST AI 600-1 (or similar) compliance | The cross-vet workflow itself, with substantial NIST AI RMF alignment via the four-function mapping below. |

The methodology gives an adopter **at least two independent paths**
to the § 552.105(e) defense — (A) via AI-reviewer feedback and
(D) via NIST-aligned internal review — plus the underlying
§ 552.105(c) rebuttable presumption of reasonable care that the
methodology's audit trail supports. That redundancy is
operationally meaningful: a deprecated AI reviewer doesn't break
(D); a discontinued NIST profile doesn't break (A).

The methodology's substantial alignment with NIST AI 600-1 maps
onto the four AI RMF functions:

| NIST AI RMF function | Methodology element |
|----------------------|---------------------|
| **GOVERN** | Methodology declaration in `CONTRIBUTING.md`; named human approver per change; critical-path category list |
| **MAP** | Per-PR critical-path category check; risk-flag entries in pair-review Section A; failure-mode incident log |
| **MEASURE** | AI reviewer automated review on every PR; pair-review tables capturing what was caught vs. missed |
| **MANAGE** | Audit trail with model versions and prompt logs; periodic competence assessments; remediation tracked through follow-up PRs (the PR #58 → #59 pattern) |

A project running the methodology has substantially done the work
§ 552.105(e)(2)(D) requires for the internal-review-process mechanism
to qualify — without having framed it that way.

The structural counterpart in civil-law jurisdictions adds support.
Under German Civil Code (BGB), interns are categorized as
**Erfüllungsgehilfen** — vicarious agents whose principals carry
liability for ordinary errors. The audit trail of supervision
determines whether the principal exercised reasonable care. This
is the same shape as the trainee-supervision pattern the
methodology mirrors — codified, in continuous use, and not at all
novel.

Two limits worth naming. TRAIGA is one US state, and § 552.105's
discovery-and-cure architecture is untested in litigation — I
cannot promise the defense holds in any specific case. The
"another nationally or internationally recognized risk management
framework" language is open-textured; whether ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/
IEC 5339 once finalized, or EU AI Act implementation guidance
qualifies is a question for counsel. Adopters should treat this
section as strong alignment with current legal direction
[13, 14, 15], not guaranteed defense.

## §4.5. Why supervised trainee, not defective product or pure agent

Courts have not converged on a single legal framing for AI — they
are picking framings by context. Three contexts have produced
distinct doctrines, and code review is structurally unlike all
three.

In **defamation** cases, courts have rejected treating LLMs as
journalists or authors who failed to fact-check; instead they apply
**product liability** — treating LLMs as defectively designed
products. This makes sense for defamation, where the harmed party
is consuming an AI-generated artifact passively. It does not fit
code review, where the AI's output is reasoned over by a downstream
reviewer rather than consumed unmodified.

In **employment discrimination** cases — the Workday line — courts
have moved toward **AI as delegated agent of employer**. The
vendor is "delegated responsibility" for hiring decisions and acts
as the employer's agent. This framing is *compatible* with the
trainee analogy; both treat AI as more than a pure tool. But it
emphasizes the *delegation* relationship rather than the
*supervision* relationship, which is the wrong emphasis for code
review where the human reviewer is doing substantive review work,
not just delegating.

In *United States v. Heppner* (Harvard Law Review, March 2026), the
court rejected the argument that Claude could function as
"a lawyer's highly trained agent" covered by attorney-client
privilege under the Kovel doctrine. Kovel-style **professional
agent** is too strong an analogy — AI lacks the licensure,
professional duties, and ethics-board accountability that the
doctrine assumes. The trainee framing avoids this overreach: an
intern is not a Kovel agent either, and the law accommodates the
distinction without difficulty.

So: not a defective product (the reviewer reasons over outputs, not
consumes them); not a pure delegated agent (the human is doing
substantive review, not delegating); not a Kovel-style professional
agent (AI lacks the underlying professional structure). What's left
is the supervised-trainee model, which fits the structure of the
work — and which existing law accommodates without requiring novel
doctrine.

## §5. Where the analogy holds and where it breaks

The trainee analogy is not a perfect fit. Honest treatment requires
naming the breaks.

**It holds for production and ownership.** Work-for-hire vests
output in the employer for paid interns who qualify as employees.
Vendor ToS plus the Copyright Office's "substantial creative input"
standard reaches the same outcome for AI use. The audit trail is the
shared evidence base for both.

**It holds for liability allocation.** Respondeat superior covers
employees including paid interns. German BGB Erfüllungsgehilfen
treatment is the civil-law analog. AI's "no personal liability
because no personhood" reaches the same end state — liability
concentrated on the principal — through different doctrine.

**It holds for the trainee standard of care.** *Jistarri v. Nappi*
(1988) established a sliding-scale trainee standard in medical
malpractice (resident held to higher standard than general
practitioner, lower than specialist). Software has no equivalent
licensure regime, but the structural principle — reduced standard
for trainees, supervisor liable for inadequate supervision — is
recognized doctrine.

**It breaks for unpaid interns.** *Hubay v. Mendez* (2020) held
that works created by unpaid volunteers and interns are generally
NOT works made for hire. My claim is specifically about *paid*
interns classified as employees. The unpaid-intern carve-out
doesn't apply to AI directly (vendor ToS handles vesting), but it
means the analogy must be precise — drop "paid" and the framing
sandbags itself on the first reviewer who notices.

**It breaks for testimony.** A live intern can be deposed; AI
cannot be cross-examined. Ordinarily this would be a serious
asymmetry. In practice, the comparison is rarely to a live intern
— it is to a departed intern, who is also unavailable, and whose
artefact (the wiki post, the PR comment, the commit message) is
admissible under the business-records exception (FRE 803(6)) without
requiring testimony. Both AI and a departed intern rely on the
time-of-work artefact plus a system to authenticate it.

**It breaks for transience.** AI proposer model versions get
deprecated; vendor contracts get terminated; underlying frameworks
change in ways that may make the same prompt no longer produce the
same output. Departed interns don't reappear either, but they at
least leave more reliably reconstructible chains of reasoning. The
audit-trail discipline I will describe in §7 is the operational
response: pin model versions, retain prompt chains, document the
exact tool stack at time of work.

**It breaks for moral attribution.** Interns get acknowledged —
informally if not formally. They get a line on a CV, a
recommendation letter, a thank-you in a paper. AI gets none of
these. The methodology's audit trail makes attribution *possible*;
prevailing convention — academic publication norms, work-for-hire
defaults — does not encourage acknowledging AI's substantive
contributions explicitly. This article tries to model a different
practice (see the authorship note above the abstract). I do not
claim the practice is settled.

**It breaks for development trajectory.** Interns grow into peers.
AI versions update via vendor releases that aren't the same
continuous individual. The asymmetric-epistemic-source argument
(AI brings external knowledge; the human reviewer brings internal
tacit context) may weaken if AI tools acquire substantial internal
knowledge through fine-tuning or persistent context. The
methodology's other supports — legal structure, audit trail —
survive that capability shift; the epistemic-complementarity
argument may not.

A counterpart case to PR #59 makes the asymmetric-source argument
concrete from the human's side. PR #60 was a refactor that
extracted shared currency-formatting helpers across the codebase.
Codex reviewed it and posted no findings — the only PR in the
origin window that came back clean. But while doing the refactor,
I found something neither AI tier had flagged across multiple
prior PRs: a long-standing dead-code dyscalculia gate, where the
dyscalculia branch added a `'/mo'` suffix that the fallback branch
didn't, leading to silent inconsistency in lump-sum displays for
years.

Why neither AI tier caught it is the interesting question. The dead
code was uniformly distributed across screens, so pattern-matching
on consistency didn't flag it. And it was pre-existing in files
Codex was reviewing only for diff changes, so it wasn't in scope
for the AI reviewer's local-to-the-diff reasoning. What surfaced it
was internal-context review: knowing that
`DyscalculiaService.formatCurrency` already handled the disabled
case (making the per-screen gate dead), knowing the codebase's
history of `'/mo'` suffix conventions, and bringing the
refactor-induced full-file reading together with that context.
This is exactly the unobservable-internal layer the asymmetric-
source argument attributes to the human role.

A third case surfaces a dimension the simple "AI-external,
human-internal" decomposition doesn't fully capture. PR #61
extracted inline Angular templates into separate `.html` and
`.scss` files for six components, following Angular style guide
rule 05-04 ("extract templates over three lines"). The human
prompted the refactor to follow the standard pattern; the AI
proposer pushed back on the scope, recommending extraction only for
components with at least 200 lines of inline template, and arguing
this directly in the PR body:

> AI agents (incl. me, in future sessions) need 2 Reads per
> migrated component instead of 1 — small cost, often offset by
> smaller individual files. Components with <200 LOC template
> intentionally NOT migrated — single-file is nicer there.

That is **the AI flagging an AI-readability concern as a constraint
on a refactor pattern that exists for human readability reasons.**
The Angular style rule was written when "future readers" meant
humans and IDEs. The AI proposer surfaced that a class of future
readers — itself, in subsequent sessions, navigating the codebase
through `Read` tool calls — has different cost dynamics. The human
arbitrated: keep the rule, narrow the scope.

This is a category of cross-vet value the simple two-axis
asymmetric-source model misses. Both sides bring readability and
maintainability concerns *as future readers of the code*. The human
reads for human review, debugging, and onboarding. The AI reads for
future AI sessions — file-load efficiency, single-file context,
context-window economy. They sometimes agree (extract very large
files); they sometimes conflict (don't extract small ones). The
methodology benefits from making both perspectives explicit.

A practical consequence: the pair-review template's Section A
should include "AI-readability concern" as a named type tag
alongside ambiguity, conflict, and risk flags. Without it, AI's
self-aware concerns about future-AI use of the code disappear into
generic "suggested refinement" entries, and the structurally
distinct nature of the concern is lost.

## §6. Failure modes

Seven modes where the methodology breaks down, drifts, or gives a
false sense of rigor. I have hit several in practice; the rest are
modes I watch for explicitly because the methodology's failure
profile depends on naming them out loud.

**Monoculture risk.** Both the AI proposer and the AI reviewer are
trained on largely overlapping corpora. If a wrong pattern is
common in training data, both treat it as correct. Mitigation:
choose proposer and reviewer from different vendors where possible.
Watch for: finding the same idiom in multiple AI tools is not
evidence the idiom is correct.

**Hallucination cascade.** AI proposer suggests something subtly
wrong; AI reviewer fails to flag; human accepts trusting green
CI plus clean automated review. The PR #60 case in §5 is
structurally similar — pre-existing wrong code that neither AI
tier flagged because it was outside their respective reasoning
windows. Mitigation: human review must engage with the substance,
not just the bot's verdict.

**Reviewer competence drift.** The human reviewer gets used to
clicking accept. The methodology degrades into rubber-stamp-by-
human with extra steps. Mitigation: quarterly self-spot-checks
where the reviewer pulls a random PR they approved and re-justifies
the merge.

**Audit-trail completeness.** Conversation transcripts and PR
threads are the evidence base. Default tool behavior evicts them —
chat clients prune old conversations, IDEs discard inline AI
suggestions, draft PRs vanish on branch deletion. If you didn't
explicitly retain it, you don't have it. The §3 finding that 6 of
8 Codex review comments have unverified resolution status is this
mode in operation.

**IP / copyright provenance.** AI suggestions may contain verbatim
training-data fragments. Vendor ToS attempts to indemnify. No
regulatory framework currently gates this. Watch for: suspiciously
specific code that "just works" — named constants matching a known
library's internals, unusual algorithms with no obvious derivation.

**Liability attribution.** If an AI-suggested bug ships and causes
harm, common-law product liability falls on whoever shipped it.
The "AI wrote it" defense does not work. The merging human owns
the change.

**Reviewer-of-AI competence is undocumented.** The methodology
assumes the human can spot AI hallucinations. No framework requires
demonstrating this skill. New joiners onboarded into the
methodology without exposure to AI failure modes are at the highest
risk of accepting confident-looking nonsense. A short internal
"AI hallucinations I have seen" curriculum is more useful than a
generic training course.

The honest answer about correlated failures — where AI proposer,
AI reviewer, and human all miss — is that the methodology reduces
but does not eliminate residual risk. The retirement-dashboard
origin sessions had several Codex catches that the AI proposer
missed, and at least one issue all three missed initially that
surfaced only on a later session's read-through. Domains with
zero-tolerance for residual risk (life-safety, financially-material)
need a layer beyond what this methodology provides — a second
human reviewer, a domain-specific qualified review tier, or both.

## §7. Adoption

Two adoption tiers. The minimum viable version is a five-line
checklist; production-grade adoption requires a five-cadence audit
artifact framework on top.

Minimum viable, to run the methodology in your repo at all:

- Use an AI coding assistant for proposals (Claude Code, Cursor,
  Copilot, etc.)
- Enable an independent automated PR reviewer (Codex, CodeRabbit,
  Anthropic Cloud Code Review, etc.)
- Require human approval on every merge — branch protection, no
  exceptions for AI bots
- Drop a methodology declaration into `CONTRIBUTING.md`
- Decide on a retention plan for PR threads and AI conversation
  transcripts

That's the floor. Production-grade adoption — adoption that holds
up against a serious audit, supports the TRAIGA-style affirmative
defense, or survives a deprecation event — requires more. The
audit-artifact framework has five cadences:

- **Per-instance** (per PR): PR thread, conversation transcript,
  AI reviewer comments, commit messages with `Co-Authored-By`
  trailers, **pair-review table with both Section A (AI notes
  about prompts) and Section B (human notes about implementation)**
- **Per-session**: session summary in vault or `docs/sessions/`,
  decision log entries for non-obvious calls, **attribution notes
  recording who or what introduced which idea**, open-questions list
- **Per-project**: methodology declaration, model and version log,
  vendor inventory, failure-mode incident log, critical-path
  category list
- **Per-period** (quarterly recommended): reviewer competence
  assessment, compliance check against the evidence-checklist,
  methodology drift check, AI tool inventory refresh
- **Per-publication**: AI contribution disclosure, argument-by-
  argument attribution map, audit trail link, methodology compliance
  attestation

The strict requirement is reconstructability from scratch: someone
reading the repo plus linked audit trail should be able to see what
AI was used, what the human approved, what was deferred, and why,
without needing to ask the original author.

The pair-review table is the single artifact that's most distinctive
to this methodology, and it is where adoption tends to drift.
Expected drift modes: humans fill Section B but skip Section A
(making AI's contributions invisible — exactly what the artifact is
supposed to fix); both sections get filled with "looks fine"
entries (performative compliance); granularity creeps from per-PR
to per-session (averaging across unrelated work).

The pair-review template ships with standardized type tags for each
section — Section A: `Ambiguity`, `Multiple matches`, `Conflict
flag`, `Risk flag`, `Scope expansion`, `Suggested refinement`;
Section B: `Style / convention`, `Logic`, `Coverage gap`,
`Performance`, `Security`, `Disagreement-but-deferred`,
`Approved-as-is`. The tags enable cross-PR analysis and serve as a
nudge against pure narrative-style entries.

Per-publication artifacts deserve specific mention because they are
where the moral-attribution question from §5 lands operationally.
For an article like this one, the audit trail records which
substantive arguments were introduced by which party, when. The
authorship note above the abstract draws from that trail. Conventional
academic publication norms do not require this; the methodology
suggests it.

## §8. Open questions and forward look

The methodology has been honest about its limits throughout. I
close with five questions I have not answered, and one prediction
about how the picture changes within 1-2 years.

**Does the methodology degrade when proposer and reviewer share a
model?** If both come from the same vendor, the monoculture risk
becomes correlated rather than independent. I have used Claude Code
plus Codex (Anthropic plus OpenAI); I have not tested
Claude-on-Claude. Empirical comparison would settle this.

**What is the right escalation when AI plus AI plus human all
miss?** The methodology is robust to single misses. Correlated
misses — where the same training-data blind spot affects all
three reasoning processes — are residual risk. Whether a fourth
review tier helps, or whether different reasoning modalities are
required, is open.

**Does the methodology scale beyond one human reviewer?** I have
practiced it as a single reviewer of a single repository. At
team scale, coordination overhead and competence-baseline drift
both grow. I do not know the methodology's behavior at scale.

**What is the minimum competence baseline for the human reviewer?**
The methodology assumes the human can spot AI hallucinations.
"Can" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Establishing a
baseline — what a reviewer must demonstrate before being trusted
with the methodology — is necessary for organizational adoption
and untested.

**When does AI graduate from intern to peer?** The trainee analogy
is structural for the current generation of AI tools. If AI
acquires substantial internal-corporate knowledge (via fine-tuning,
persistent context, or organizational integration), the asymmetric-
epistemic-source argument from §1 weakens. The methodology then has
to rest on the legal-structural argument and the audit-trail
discipline alone. Whether that's enough is open.

Within 1-2 years, several regulatory developments will reshape the
picture: ISO/IEC 5339 (AI-augmented SDLC standards), NIST SSDF v2
(updated for generative AI), EU AI Act Code of Practice
implementation guidance, and likely additional US state laws. The
methodology will need to update accordingly — the audit-trail
discipline is what makes that update tractable. A repository
running the methodology can answer "what AI tools did we use, when,
under what oversight" by reading its own audit trail; a repository
without the discipline answers that question by archeology.

The methodology is short to describe. The interesting work is
running it consistently long enough that the audit trail becomes
load-bearing — and noticing what stops working when the practice
slips. This article is one repository's first month of trying.
Other practitioners running the methodology in different contexts
will find different failure modes, different competing framings,
and (I hope) different load-bearing arguments. The methodology's
own audit-trail discipline is what makes those findings comparable.
